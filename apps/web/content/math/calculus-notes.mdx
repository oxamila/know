Calculus is the mathematics of **change** and **accumulation**. It answers two deceptively simple
questions:

1. **How fast is something changing right now?** → *differential calculus* (derivatives)
2. **How much accumulates in total?** → *integral calculus* (integrals)

The stunning surprise — the **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus** — is that these two questions are
inverse operations of each other. Every graph on this page is live: drag to pan, scroll to zoom.

## Limits — the foundation of everything

Before we can speak of "instantaneous" change, we need the idea of a **limit**: what value a
function *approaches* as $x$ approaches some point $c$, even if the function is undefined there.

$$
\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L
$$

The classic example is $f(x) = \frac{\sin x}{x}$. At $x = 0$ the formula divides by zero — yet the
function clearly approaches $1$. Zoom in near the origin (scroll on the graph) and watch the hole
at $x = 0$ get arbitrarily close to height $1$:

The point at $x=0$ is simply missing (the function is undefined there) — the limit still exists.
This "approach without arriving" idea is what makes derivatives possible.

## Derivatives (differentiation)

### The tangent-line problem

What is the slope of $y = x^2$ exactly at the point $(1, 1)$? A slope needs two points, but a
tangent touches only one. Calculus' trick: **take secant lines with a second point closer and
closer, and watch the slopes converge**.

Below, $f(x) = x^2$ is drawn with secant lines from $(1, 1)$ to $(1+h, f(1+h))$ for $h = 1$,
$0.5$, $0.1$ — and the limiting tangent. Compare how each secant's slope ($3$, then $2.5$, then
$2.1$) creeps toward the tangent's slope, $2$:

### The formal definition

The derivative is exactly that limiting slope, for *every* point $x$:

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

The expression $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$ is the **difference quotient** — the slope of a secant
over a step of size $h$. Sending $h \to 0$ squashes the secant onto the tangent. For $f(x) = x^2$
this computation is worth doing once by hand:

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} (2x + h) = 2x
$$

### The derivative as a function

Once we have $f'(x)$ for every $x$, we can graph $f$ and $f'$ **on the same axes** and read the
relationship directly: wherever $f'$ is *above* zero, $f$ is *rising*; wherever $f'$ dips below
zero, $f$ is *falling*; and $f$ flattens out exactly where $f'$ crosses the axis.

Follow the blue curve from left to right and check it against the pink derivative:

- On $(-\infty, -1)$: $f'$ is positive → $f$ rises.
- At $x = -1$: $f' = 0$ → $f$ peaks (a local maximum).
- On $(-1, 1)$: $f'$ is negative → $f$ falls.
- At $x = 1$: $f' = 0$ → $f$ bottoms out (a local minimum).
- On $(1, \infty)$: $f'$ is positive → $f$ rises again.

This is **curve sketching from derivatives** — the mechanical heart of differential calculus.

### Differentiation rules

Computing derivatives from the limit definition every time would be unbearable. The rules below
turn it into algebra. (Try verifying each one against a graph of $f$ and $f'$.)

| Rule | Statement | Example |
| ---- | --------- | ------- |
| Constant | $\frac{d}{dx} c = 0$ | $\frac{d}{dx} 7 = 0$ |
| Power rule | $\frac{d}{dx} x^n = n x^{n-1}$ | $\frac{d}{dx} x^5 = 5x^4$ |
| Constant multiple | $\frac{d}{dx} [c \cdot f(x)] = c \cdot f'(x)$ | $\frac{d}{dx} 3x^2 = 6x$ |
| Sum rule | $\frac{d}{dx} [f + g] = f' + g'$ | $\frac{d}{dx}(x^2 + \sin x) = 2x + \cos x$ |
| Product rule | $\frac{d}{dx}[f \cdot g] = f'g + fg'$ | $\frac{d}{dx}(x e^x) = e^x + x e^x$ |
| Chain rule | $\frac{d}{dx} f(g(x)) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$ | $\frac{d}{dx} \sin(x^2) = 2x\cos(x^2)$ |

The **chain rule** is the one that repays deep study: it is how calculus handles nested change —
the rate of the outer thing, times the rate of the inner thing.

## Integration (integral calculus)

### The area problem

Flip the tangent-line question around. Instead of *given a curve, find the slope*, ask: *given a
rate, find the total*. Concretely: **what is the area between $y = \sin x$ and the $x$-axis from
$0$ to $\pi$?** The shaded region below *is* the definite integral

$$
\int_0^{\pi} \sin x \, dx
$$

The arch of sine from $0$ to $\pi$ encloses exactly **2** square units. (Scroll to inspect the

### Riemann sums — approximating area with rectangles

How would you *compute* that area from scratch? Cut the interval into thin slices, build
rectangles, add their areas, and take more, thinner slices. With a **left Riemann sum** of $n$
rectangles on $[a, b]$:

$$
\text{Area} \approx \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} f(x_i)\,\Delta x, \qquad \Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}
$$

Below: $f(x) = x^2$ on $[0, 2]$ — bars are the 8-rectangle left sum (total $\approx 2.75$), the
pink area is the true integral $\int_0^2 x^2\,dx = \tfrac{8}{3} \approx 2.667$. Notice every bar's
top-left corner touches the curve, so the bars *overshoot* on the rising half — exactly the
error a finer partition fixes:

Letting $n \to \infty$ (rectangles become infinitely thin) turns the approximation into the
**definite integral** — that limiting process *defines* $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$.

### Indefinite integrals — anti-differentiation

An **antiderivative** of $f$ is any function $F$ with $F' = f$. The **indefinite integral**

$$
\int f(x)\, dx = F(x) + C
$$

names the whole *family* of antiderivatives — one for each choice of the constant $C$, because
differentiating kills constants. For $f(x) = 2x$:

$$
\int 2x\, dx = x^2 + C
$$

All the curves $x^2 + C$ are **parallel** — identical shapes slid vertically — and *every one of
them* has slope $2x$ at each $x$. Compare the family against the shared derivative:

    { func: (x) => x * x, color: '#10b981', label: 'x² (C = 0)' },
    { func: (x) => x * x + 2, color: '#8b5cf6', label: 'x² + 2 (C = +2)' },

At any fixed $x$, all three blue/purple/green curves have the *same* slope — the red value
$2x$. That uniformity across the family is exactly what "the antiderivative is only defined up
to a constant" means geometrically.

Common antiderivatives (each verifiable by differentiating the right-hand side):

| $f(x)$ | $\int f(x)\,dx$ |
| ------ | --------------- |
| $x^n$ $(n \neq -1)$ | $\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$ |
| $\frac{1}{x}$ | $\ln\|x\| + C$ |
| $e^x$ | $e^x + C$ |
| $\sin x$ | $-\cos x + C$ |
| $\cos x$ | $\sin x + C$ |
| $c \cdot f(x)$ | $c \int f(x)\,dx$ |

## The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Here is the bridge between the two halves. Differentiation and integration are **inverse
operations**.

### Part 1 — the area function is an antiderivative

Define the **accumulation function**

$$
A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\, dt
$$

(the shaded area from a fixed $a$ up to a moving endpoint $x$). Then:

$$
A'(x) = f(x)
$$

**Rates and totals undo each other.** The rate at which area accumulates *is* the height of the
curve. Watch it concretely with $f(t) = \sin t$ on $[0, x]$: its area function is
$A(x) = \int_0^x \sin t\, dt = 1 - \cos x$ — and differentiating $1 - \cos x$ returns $\sin x$,
as promised:

  functions={[

    { func: (x) => 1 - Math.cos(x), color: '#ec4899', label: 'A(x) = 1 − cos(x)' },
  ]}

/>

Read the graph: while the blue curve is positive ($0$ to $\pi$), the pink area function *climbs*.
While blue is negative ($\pi$ to $2\pi$), pink *descends* — subtracting area. Pink flattens
precisely where blue crosses zero. **The graph of $A$ is the running total of $f$; the graph of
$f$ is the slope of $A$.**

### Part 2 — computing areas with antiderivatives

Part 2 is the computational payoff. If $F' = f$, then

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = F(b) - F(a)
$$

No rectangles, no limits — just evaluate an antiderivative at the two endpoints. For our sine
arch:

$$
\int_0^{\pi} \sin x\, dx = [-\cos x]_0^{\pi} = (-\cos \pi) - (-\cos 0) = 1 + 1 = 2
$$

And for the parabola:

$$
\int_0^2 x^2\, dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2 = \frac{8}{3} - 0 = \frac{8}{3}
$$

Both match the shaded areas in the graphs above — the theorem earned its name.

### Putting all three curves together

One function, three calculus companions: $f$, its derivative $f'$, and its area function $F$
(with $F(x) = \int_0^x f$). For $f(x) = \cos x$ we have $F(x) = \sin x$:

phase by a quarter wavelength; integration rotates it back the other way.**

## Worked examples

### Example 1 — differentiate a polynomial

**Problem.** Find $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = 3x^4 - 2x + 5$, and locate its critical points.

**Solution.** Apply the power and sum rules term by term:

$$
f'(x) = 12x^3 - 2
$$

Critical points where $f' = 0$: $12x^3 = 2$, so $x = \sqrt[3]{1/6} \approx 0.55$. Verify on the

### Example 2 — the chain rule in the wild

**Problem.** Differentiate $g(x) = \sin(x^2)$.

**Solution.** Outer function $\sin(\cdot)$, inner function $u = x^2$. By the chain rule:

$$
g'(x) = \cos(x^2) \cdot 2x = 2x\cos(x^2)
$$

### Example 3 — a definite integral by hand

**Problem.** Compute $\int_1^3 (2x + 1)\, dx$ geometrically and with the FTC.

**Solution.** The region is a trapezoid under the line $y = 2x+1$ from $1$ to $3$ — heights $3$
and $7$, width $2$ — area $\tfrac{3+7}{2} \cdot 2 = 10$. With the FTC, antiderivative $x^2 + x$:

$$
\int_1^3 (2x+1)\,dx = [x^2 + x]_1^3 = (9+3) - (1+1) = 10
$$

### Example 4 — optimization with derivatives

**Problem.** A farmer has 40 m of fence for three sides of a rectangular pen (a river forms the
fourth side). Maximize the enclosed area.

**Solution.** Let $x$ be each side perpendicular to the river; the parallel side is
$40 - 2x$. Area:

$$
A(x) = x(40 - 2x) = 40x - 2x^2, \qquad A'(x) = 40 - 4x
$$

Setting $A' = 0$ gives $x = 10$ — the unique critical point, and since $A'' = -4 < 0$ it is a
maximum. The optimal pen is $10 \times 20$ m for $200\ \mathrm{m}^2$. The parabola below peaks
exactly where its derivative crosses zero:

## The big picture

| Question | Tool | Symbol | Answer type |
| -------- | ---- | ------ | ----------- |
| Slope / instantaneous rate | Derivative | $f'(x) = \dfrac{d}{dx}f(x)$ | A function |
| Antiderivative / reverse slope | Indefinite integral | $\displaystyle\int f(x)\,dx$ | A family $F(x) + C$ |
| Accumulated total / area | Definite integral | $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ | A number |
| Link between all three | FTC | $A'(x) = f(x)$, $\int_a^b f = F(b)-F(a)$ | — |

The one-sentence version: **differentiation measures change, integration adds up change, and the
Fundamental Theorem says they are inverses.** Everything else — related rates, differential
equations, series, multivariable calculus — is elaboration on that single idea.
